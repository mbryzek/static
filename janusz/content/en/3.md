3. REVIEW OF METHODS OF COMPENSATING THE EFFECT OF TEMPERATURE USING COMPENSATION CIRCUITRY IN THE ANALOG PART OF THE SIGNAL-CONDITIONING CIRCUIT

3.1. Introduction

The known circuit-compensation designs [1, 32–77] may be divided into three groups. The first group consists of designs in which the sensor for a non-electrical quantity is used at the same time as a temperature sensor. The second group is formed by designs in which, in order to sense the temperature of the sensor, components with a temperature-dependent resistance are used. In the third group of designs the temperature of the sensor is sensed by junction semiconductor devices.

Within the second and third groups we may further make a division according to the technology in which the temperature-sensitive elements are made, namely the use of discrete and of integrated components.

In the further part of this work examples of the most interesting designs will be presented.

3.2. Method using the pressure sensor as a temperature sensor

The method [61] developed at IAP PW (Institute of Industrial Automation, Warsaw University of Technology) was applied to the measurement of hydrostatic pressure, and of the temperature in the region in which that pressure acts, by means of a single sensor consisting of a semiconductor p-n junction with an arbitrary breakdown mechanism, supplied with an alternating square-wave current. The forward voltage \(U_{p}\) and the reverse voltage \(U_{z}\) are expressed by the following relations:

$$ U_{p} = U_{po} \cdot \left[ 1 + \alpha_{p} \cdot (p - p_{o}) - \beta_{p} \cdot (T - T_{o}) \right] $$

$$ U_{z} = U_{zo} \cdot \left[ 1 + \alpha_{z} \cdot (p - p_{o}) + \beta_{z} \cdot (T - T_{o}) \right] $$

where \(U_{po}\) and \(U_{zo}\) denote respectively the forward voltage

<!-- scan p022 | printed page 19 | type: equations -->

and the reverse voltage at the lower-range pressure \(p_{o}\) and the reference temperature \(T_{o}\), \(\alpha_{p}\) and \(\alpha_{z}\) denote the pressure sensitivity of the junction in the forward and in the reverse direction respectively, while \(\beta_{p}\) and \(\beta_{z}\) denote respectively the temperature coefficient of the voltage in the forward and in the reverse direction respectively.

[TN: the symbol \(U_{p}\) denotes the forward voltage in this section only; from Section 4 onward \(U_{p}\) is the transducer output at the lower range value. Both uses are kept as printed.]

The sign "+" or "-" of the pressure sensitivities of the junction depends on the material from which the junction is made. The sign "+" or "-" of the temperature coefficient \(\beta_{z}\) of the reverse voltage depends on the breakdown mechanism (Zener or avalanche). The sign of the temperature coefficient \(\beta_{p}\) of the forward voltage is always negative. The coefficients \(\alpha_{p}\), \(\alpha_{z}\), \(\beta_{p}\), \(\beta_{z}\) are a function of the current flowing through the junction.

By forming, with the aid of an electronic circuit with memory (Fig. 3), the sum \(U^{+}\) and the difference \(U^{-}\) of the forward and reverse voltages with the appropriate proportionality coefficients \(k_{p}\), \(k_{z}\) respectively, one obtains the possibility of making this sum or difference insensitive to changes of pressure or to changes of temperature

$$ \frac{\partial U^{+}}{\partial T} = 0 \qquad \text{or} \qquad \frac{\partial U^{+}}{\partial p} = 0 $$

and

$$ \frac{\partial U^{-}}{\partial T} = 0 \qquad \text{or} \qquad \frac{\partial U^{-}}{\partial p} = 0 $$

In the case where \(\alpha_{p}\), \(\alpha_{z}\), \(\beta_{p}\), \(\beta_{z}\) are not a function of temperature and pressure, two of the four relations above can be satisfied, and one obtains a pressure-compensated temperature transducer or a temperature-compensated pressure transducer.

The compensation method presented in [71], proposed by Statham (USA), consists in connecting two-terminal networks with a temperature-dependent resistance in series with the arms of the strain-gauge bridge, or in parallel with them, in order to compensate the zero drift, and in connecting analogous two-terminal networks in series with the bridge, or in series-parallel with it, in order to compensate the span.

The compensation consists in a variable loading of the bridge output. The circuit in Fig. 4b was modified by introducing a stronger division of the output signal by means of the resistor \(R_{5}\).

Another design of the compensation method was introduced by Kulite (USA) [77], Fig. 6.

In order to temperature-compensate the zero of the strain-gauge bridge built from the strain gauges \(R_{1}\) to \(R_{4}\), the voltage between the points A and C is equalized at the reference temperature with the voltage between the points A and B. The value of the required current flowing through the resistors \(R_{9}\) and \(R_{10}\) depends on the value of their resistance and does not change the balance condition of the strain-gauge bridge at the reference temperature.

and the value of the voltage at the output of the circuit is measured at the lower and at the upper value of the range of the measured pressure. At the compensation temperature the values of the resistors \(R_{9}\), \(R_{10}\), and then \(R_{6}\), are set so as to obtain the previously measured values of the measured signal.

An interesting design was proposed in [37], developed at MIFI (Moscow Engineering Physics Institute), USSR. The electronic circuit for applying the method is shown in Fig. 7. The strain-gauge bridge built from the strain gauges \(R_{1}\) to \(R_{4}\) is supplied through the resistor \(R_{5}\). The compensation of the zero drift is carried out by means of the auxiliary bridge M containing the thermistor RT. At the reference temperature the voltage across the diagonal AB of the bridge is equal to zero (which is obtained by means of the potentiometer P2), and the zero adjustment of the transducer is performed with the potentiometer P1. At the compensation temperature the output signal of the amplifier W1 is brought back to its previous value by adding to the sensor signal the output signal of the amplifier W2 with a weighting factor set by the resistor \(R_{12}\) or \(R_{13}\). The output signal of the amplifier W2 is made temperature-dependent by means of the auxiliary bridge M.

The span compensation is realized by means of the resistor \(R_{5}\) and by making use of the variable resistance of the strain gauges \(R_{1}\) to \(R_{4}\) as a function of temperature, which causes changes of the voltage \(U_{AB}\) across the bridge.

The method allows a relatively simple two-point compensation of the zero drift and a coarse compensation of the span. It requires, however, the use of an additional operational amplifier, which is its drawback.

3.4. Methods using discrete junction semiconductor devices as temperature sensors.

The designs presented below make use of the linear dependence on temperature of the forward voltage of a diode or of the base-emitter voltage of a transistor. At the same time, variable-resistance components may be used for the compensation.

In the method used at Mera-Pnefal, Warsaw (Poland) [33], the compensation of the zero drift is realized by means of resistive components (Fig. 8). In the measuring bridge two strain gauges \(R_{3}\) and \(R_{4}\) are placed in opposite arms. The values of the resistors \(R_{5}\) and \(R_{7}\) are made independent of temperature, while the values of the resistors \(R_{6}\) and \(R_{8}\) have a positive temperature coefficient of resistance. By measuring the total resistance \(R_{5} + R_{6}\) and \(R_{7} + R_{8}\) required for nulling the bridge at two temperatures one obtains the information necessary to calculate the resistors \(R_{5}\) to \(R_{8}\), where one of the resistors \(R_{7}\), \(R_{8}\) is always equal to zero. The potentiometer P serves for the precise nulling of the bridge.

The advantage of designs using integrated temperature-sensing elements is the smaller temperature gradients between these elements and the sensor, resulting from the small thermal resistance between these elements.

3.6. Method using integrated semiconductor devices as temperature sensors

The conceptually most interesting compensation method was developed by National Semiconductor (USA) [1, 42, 70]. This method is closely tied to the integrated pressure sensor also developed at that company. This sensor is built from four strain gauges \(R_{1}\), \(R_{2}\), \(R_{3}\), \(R_{4}\) (Fig. 13) diffused into the silicon sensing diaphragm. Between these strain gauges the transistor T4 is diffused. Into the same silicon chip, outside the region of the diaphragm, the transistors T1, T2, T3 and the operational amplifier W are also diffused. All the diffused components are located close to one another, owing to which the thermal resistance between them is very small.

The transistor T4 is used as a heater controlled by a square wave from a drive generator. The rise time of the temperature from 298 K to 423 K of all the diffused components, with the exception of the amplifier W, is about 200 ms (thermal time constant approx. 100 ms, thermal resistance approx. 300 K/W). As a result it becomes possible to change the temperature of the diffused components at a frequency of the order of a few Hz.

The first element subjected to temperature compensation in the sensor circuit is the supply voltage between the points A and B. This voltage is supplied by a Zener diode formed by the reverse-biased base-emitter junction of the transistor T2, with a voltage of approx. 6.4 V and a temperature coefficient of approx. +2.5 mV/K. In the method presented, however, temperature gradients cannot be avoided, in consequence of which the compensation accuracy is limited. Nevertheless the method makes possible the full automation of the compensation procedure. The unit price of this company's complete pressure transducers is $10 in lots of 10 000 units, so they are several tens of times cheaper than the transducers of all other competing companies.

3.7. Summary

The known temperature-compensation methods presented above may be characterized as follows:
(a) none of the known methods makes complete compensation of the temperature errors possible. In almost all the methods only one compensation temperature is used, with the exception of the Honeywell method with three-point compensation, i.e. with two temperatures;
(b) only one method, developed by Kulite, ensures unidirectional independence in setting the compensation conditions, since the values of the resistors \(R_{6}\), \(R_{9}\), \(R_{10}\) (Fig. 4) adjusted at the compensation temperature do not affect the signal of the transducer at the reference temperature;
(c) in each of the methods presented, the number of values of the compensating components necessary to compensate all the sensors

   in a production lot is large. In all the designs in which the compensating components are connected in series with the strain gauges, great difficulties arise, either in making or in purchasing stable components with values of the order of a few ohms or fractions of an ohm, which always go together with the use of low-resistance gauges (e.g. 100 Ω);
(d) the compensation procedure based on all the known methods, with the exception of the method developed by Kulite, is difficult and burdensome under production conditions;
(e) the conceptually most interesting method, that of National Semiconductor, requires repeating the temperature cycles many times, which, because of the time required, is unfeasible if another technology of manufacturing the transducer is used, and also because of the temperature gradients that occur if the requirements as to the compensation accuracy are raised.
