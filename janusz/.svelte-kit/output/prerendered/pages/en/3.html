3. REVIEW OF METHODS OF COMPENSATING THE EFFECT OF TEMPERATURE USING COMPENSATION CIRCUITRY IN THE ANALOG PART OF THE SIGNAL-CONDITIONING CIRCUIT

s. 18

3.1. Introduction

The known circuit-compensation designs [1, 32–77] may be divided into three groups. The first group consists of designs in which the sensor for a non-electrical quantity is used at the same time as a temperature sensor. The second group is formed by designs in which, in order to sense the temperature of the sensor, components with a temperature-dependent resistance are used. In the third group of designs the temperature of the sensor is sensed by junction semiconductor devices.

Within the second and third groups we may further make a division according to the technology in which the temperature-sensitive elements are made, namely the use of discrete and of integrated components.

In the further part of this work examples of the most interesting designs will be presented.

3.3. Method using discrete variable-resistance components to sense temperature

The compensation method presented in [71], proposed by Statham (USA), consists in connecting two-terminal networks with a temperature-dependent resistance in series with the arms of the strain-gauge bridge, or in parallel with them, in order to compensate the zero drift, and in connecting analogous two-terminal networks in series with the bridge, or in series-parallel with it, in order to compensate the span.

3.4. Methods using discrete junction semiconductor devices as temperature sensors.

The designs presented below make use of the linear dependence on temperature of the forward voltage of a diode or of the base-emitter voltage of a transistor. At the same time, variable-resistance components may be used for the compensation.

In the method used at Mera-Pnefal (Poland) [33], the compensation of the zero drift is realized by means of resistive components (Fig. 8). In the measuring bridge two strain gauges R3R_{3}R3​ and R4R_{4}R4​ are placed in opposite arms. The values of the resistors R5R_{5}R5​ and R7R_{7}R7​ are made independent of temperature, while the values of the resistors R6R_{6}R6​ and R8R_{8}R8​ have a positive temperature coefficient of resistance. The potentiometer P serves for the precise nulling of the bridge.

3.5. Methods using integrated resistive components as temperature sensors

In the method [48], whose realization is shown in Fig. 11, developed by Bailey (USA), three resistive components R1R_{1}R1​, R2R_{2}R2​, R3R_{3}R3​, diffused into the silicon sensing diaphragm of the pressure transducer, are used. Only one component, R2R_{2}R2​, is sensitive to pressure, whereas all three change their resistance as a function of temperature. The measuring bridge was formed by connecting the piezoresistor R2R_{2}R2​ in series with the current source built on the transistor T1 in one arm, and by the series connection of the resistor R3R_{3}R3​ with the resistor R4R_{4}R4​ and the current source built on the transistor T2 in the other arm. The signal is zeroed by means of the potentiometer P.6

The temperature compensation of the zero drift was accomplished by selecting an appropriate value of the temperature-stable resistor R4R_{4}R4​, which sets the resultant temperature coefficient of the voltage in the right-hand arm of the bridge equal to the temperature coefficient of the voltage across the strain gauge. Since in the bridge it is the absolute voltage increments that compensate one another, the voltage increments in the two arms also depend on the ratio of the currents produced by the transistors T1 and T2. This makes it possible to compensate the zero drift of the transducer both when the strain gauge R2R_{2}R2​ changes more strongly as a function of temperature than the resistor R3R_{3}R3​, and in the opposite case.

3.6. Method using integrated semiconductor devices as temperature sensors

The conceptually most interesting compensation method was developed by National Semiconductor (USA) [1, 42, 70]. This method is closely tied to the integrated pressure sensor also developed at that company. This sensor is built from four strain gauges R1R_{1}R1​, R2R_{2}R2​, R3R_{3}R3​, R4R_{4}R4​ (Fig. 13) diffused into the silicon sensing diaphragm. Between these strain gauges the transistor T4 is diffused. Into the same silicon chip, outside the region of the diaphragm, the transistors T1, T2, T3 and the operational amplifier W are also diffused. All the diffused components are located close to one another, owing to which the thermal resistance between them is very small.9

The transistor T4 is used as a heater controlled by a square wave from a drive generator. The rise time of the temperature from 298 K to 423 K of all the diffused components, with the exception of the amplifier W, is about 200 ms (thermal time constant approx. 100 ms, thermal resistance approx. 300 K/W). As a result it becomes possible to change the temperature of the diffused components at a frequency of the order of a few Hz.

The first element subjected to temperature compensation in the sensor circuit is the supply voltage between the points A and B. This voltage is supplied by a Zener diode formed by the reverse-biased base-emitter junction of the transistor T2, with a voltage of approx. 6.4 V and a temperature coefficient of approx. +2.5 mV/K. The temperature compensation is obtained by means of the so-called "n⋅VBEn \cdot V_{BE}n⋅VBE​" circuit (a VBEV_{BE}VBE​ multiplier) - a voltage

Owing to the very rapid changes of temperature, this method is free of the limitations of application found in the classical methods, which are bound up with the impossibility of treating the compensation iteratively because of the required thermal soak time (approx. 1.5 h) in the temperature chamber, necessary for the temperatures to settle and equalize. The sensor discussed above which is subjected to compensation has the dimensions 2.9 × 4.2 × 0.6 mm, and the thickness of the silicon sensing diaphragm is 25 µm. The material used in the sensor - silicon - is moreover characterized by the absence of mechanical and temperature hysteresis, owing to which such rapid changes of temperature are possible.

In the method presented, however, temperature gradients cannot be avoided, in consequence of which the compensation accuracy is limited. Nevertheless the method makes possible the full automation of the compensation procedure. The unit price of this company's complete pressure transducers is $10 in lots of 10 000 units, so they are several tens of times cheaper than the transducers of all other competing companies.

3.7. Summary

The known temperature-compensation methods presented above may be characterized as follows: (a) none of the known methods makes complete compensation of the temperature errors possible. In almost all the methods only one compensation temperature is used, with the exception of the Honeywell method with three-point compensation, i.e. with two temperatures; (b) only one method, developed by Kulite, ensures unidirectional independence in setting the compensation conditions, since the values of the resistors R6R_{6}R6​, R9R_{9}R9​, R10R_{10}R10​ (Fig. 4) adjusted at the compensation temperature do not affect the signal of the transducer at the reference temperature; (c) in each of the methods presented, the number of values of the compensating components necessary to compensate all the sensors

s. 37

in a production lot is large. In all the designs in which the compensating components are connected in series with the strain gauges, great difficulties arise, either in making or in purchasing stable components with values of the order of a few ohms or fractions of an ohm, which always go together with the use of low-resistance gauges (e.g. 100 Ω); (d) the compensation procedure based on all the known methods, with the exception of the method developed by Kulite, is difficult and burdensome under production conditions; (e) the conceptually most interesting method, that of National Semiconductor, requires repeating the temperature cycles many times, which, because of the time required, is unfeasible if another technology of manufacturing the transducer is used, and also because of the temperature gradients that occur if the requirements as to the compensation accuracy are raised.10

In view of the above, in the author's opinion it appears expedient to develop a new method of compensation of the effect of temperature in semiconductor pressure transducers, one which eliminates the drawbacks of the methods known and used hitherto.
